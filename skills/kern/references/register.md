# The register

Register is how loud a design is allowed to be: how much picture, motion, depth, texture and play it carries. It is a decision, made from the brief, before any visual decision. Most generated work never makes it. It ships one register for everything, a polite middle with a fade-up, and every product comes out the same size.

A tax filing tool and a festival site should not differ only in their accent colour. One should be nearly silent and the other should be the loudest thing on the visitor's screen that day. Taste includes knowing which one you are making.

## Two ways to get it wrong

- **Too quiet for the subject.** A florist with no flowers, a racing team with no speed, a game with no play, an architect with no buildings. The page has good manners and nothing to look at. This is the [avoidance aesthetic](eye.md#two-failure-modes), and Kern itself can fall into it if you treat restraint as taste. Restraint is only taste when the subject asks for it.
- **Too loud for the use.** A 3D hero on an internal admin tool, particles behind a pricing table, a glitching headline on a pension calculator, a scroll story in front of the one number someone came for. The spectacle is real effort spent on the wrong thing.

Both are the same mistake: the register came from habit instead of the subject.

## Reading the register from the brief

If the person told you how loud it should be in [the brief](brief.md), that is the register; use the questions below only to choose its materials. Otherwise write one line for each in the record, then name the register.

1. **What is being offered: an experience or a utility?** If people come for the experience itself (food, travel, music, fashion, games, art, events, places, objects people covet), the design has to carry some of that experience. If they come to get a job done, the design gets out of the way.
2. **How often, and in what state?** Seen once by a stranger in a browser tab can afford a first impression. Used for six hours a day cannot afford anything that is not information. A worried person (a clinic, a bank, an outage) needs calm before anything else.
3. **How sensory is the subject itself?** Some subjects are made of colour, texture, sound, depth or motion: a vineyard, a synthesiser, a mountain race, a textile mill, a tracker for chiptune music. Others are made of rules and numbers. The richer the subject, the more of it should reach the screen.
4. **What does the domain already look like at its best?** Look at the domain's own most loved artefacts: record sleeves, race posters, seed catalogues, flight strips, ski maps, sneaker boxes, field guides. Their loudness is a strong hint. A domain that already makes beautiful printed things expects you to as well.
5. **What can you actually make convincing?** A register you cannot execute is worse than a lower one done well. A half-convincing 3D model loses to a strong photograph, and a weak photograph loses to strong type.

## The registers

These are positions on one dial, not styles to choose from. Two products at the same register can look nothing alike.

- **Silent.** Tools worked in all day, settings, forms, anything a stressed person uses. Native controls, content-only colour, motion only as feedback and continuity. The craft is all in density, type and states.
- **Composed.** Most product sites, docs, dashboards and apps. One clear voice, real imagery where it exists, a motion layer that responds and explains, and one small signature move.
- **Expressive.** Brands that sell a feeling as much as a function: food, hospitality, culture, consumer hardware, creative tools, events. Pictures lead, type is used as image, a real signature motion, texture and print-like treatments, one live interactive element with play in it.
- **Spectacular.** The subject is itself spatial, kinetic or theatrical, and the page is an encounter with it: a race over real terrain, a product people want to turn over, a game, a launch, an album, an exhibition. Live 3D, scroll-driven scenes, sound, full-screen motion, generative or ASCII-rendered worlds. Spent in one place, engineered to stay fast and accessible.

Pick the register, write the reason, and then **spend it**. An expressive brief delivered at composed volume is as wrong as the reverse. If you named the register expressive and the render looks like a SaaS page, you did not commit.

## The richness inventory

These are the materials a design can be made of beyond type and colour. Before composing, go through the list and decide for each one, from the ground and the register, whether this product uses it and how. Write the decisions in the record under **Register**. Most products use two or three. Leaving one out is fine when it is a decision.

| Material | Use it when | The specific version comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **Photography** | Something real exists to be seen: a place, a dish, an object, people, work | The project's own pictures, a generated brief, or credited open collections ([kern-imagery](../../kern-imagery/SKILL.md)) |
| **Archival and found images** | The domain has a visual history: plates, maps, engravings, catalogues, patents | Open collections, credited, treated as one family |
| **Illustration** | What needs showing does not exist yet, is internal, or is too abstract to photograph | A grammar derived from the domain's own drawings (technical, botanical, cartographic, comic) |
| **Image treatment** | The pictures need to become one family or carry the brand's voice | Duotone, halftone, dither, grain, overprint, cut-out, collage, masks: chosen from the domain's printing or capture technology |
| **Type as image** | The name or one word carries the idea, or pictures would be dishonest | Huge scale, cropping, variable axes, kinetic setting, the domain's own lettering |
| **Texture and material** | The domain is physical: paper, cloth, metal, film, chalk, water | A real material sampled or drawn, never noise added for "warmth" |
| **Motion** | Something changes, arrives, connects or is alive in the domain | The product's physics ([kern-motion](../../kern-motion/SKILL.md)) |
| **Text-mode and ASCII** | The product lives in a terminal, a character grid, a protocol, a data stream, or a culture that made art from characters | The real medium of the product: its output, its notation, its screen |
| **Live 3D** | The subject is spatial and moving the viewpoint reveals something | A model, terrain, assembly or space you can make convincing |
| **Generative or data art** | The product has real data or a real process with a shape | The data itself, drawn: routes, waveforms, weather, traffic, growth |
| **Sound** | The product is sound, or sound is its feedback | Real samples or synthesis, always off until asked for |
| **Play** | The visitor learns faster by doing than by reading | A real piece of the product: a configurator, a playable pattern, a slider with consequences |
| **Character** | The brand is character-led, or a guide reduces fear in the task | The product's world, rigged properly ([kern-imagery](../../kern-imagery/SKILL.md#characters-and-mascots)) |

## The relevance test

For every rich element you add, answer all three. If any answer is no, cut it or change it until it passes.

1. **Is it the product's own material?** The terrain the race runs over, the pattern the tracker plays, the plate from the seed catalogue. If it could be moved to an unrelated site unchanged, it is decoration, and decoration is the median.
2. **Does it carry information or pleasure that the still version lacks?** Turning the terrain shows the climb. Hearing the pattern shows the tool. A spinning logo shows nothing.
3. **Can you make it convincing at this register?** Credible models, real pictures, tuned timing, full frame rate on a mid-range phone, a composed fallback.

Passing all three is how you know an element belongs there, rather than asking whether it is fashionable or whether it is banned. ASCII art on a page about a terminal tracker passes. ASCII glyph rain behind a fintech headline fails. A 3D terrain under a mountain race passes. A glass torus beside a payroll headline fails.

## Variety is a result, not a goal

Never add richness to look different. Different products come out different because their registers, materials and relevance answers differ. If two unrelated briefs reach the same register with the same materials and the same treatment, one of them was not read closely enough. The ledger records the register and materials for exactly this reason.
